Real Property - Land, anything attached or affixed to the land, and anything incidental or appurtenant to the land. (Also called realty or real estate)
Personal Property - Everything that is not land or permanently attached or appurtenant to land. Usually movable. (Also called chattels)
Bundle or Rights - Right to possess, use, enjoyu, encumber, will, sell, or do nothing at all with the property
Inverted Pyramid - Within boundaries of her property, a real property owner's ownership rights apply to the surface of the earth. Those also apply to everything in an imaginary inverted pyramid, with its tip at the center of the earth, and its base in the upper regions of the sky.
Appurtenance - Goes with or pertains to ownership of a piece of real property, but isn't necessarily a physical part of the property. (Rights-of-way, easements, water rights...)
## Water Rights
Riparian Rights - Tied to land ownership. Landowner has the right to use the water that touches her property. 
Riparian Water - Flowing water, such as water in a river.
Littoral Water - Standing water, such as pond or lake (even to tidal waters like the ocean)
- Water may be used only on the riparian or littoral property itself
Prior Appropriation System - Someone who wants to use water from a river, lake, or other source must apply to the state for an appropriation permit.
- If granted, permit holder gains right to take or divert water from a particular source for a specified reasonable and beneficial use. Permit holder isn't required to own riparian or littoral land, and the permit may allow the appropriated water to be transported for use far from its source. (In WA, can be applied to groundwater beneath a property)
## Mineral Rights
They are separable and divisible.
Oil and gas governed by rule of **capture**. Any oil or gas prodcued from wells located on a landowner's property become the personal property of that landowner. (True even if wells caused the oil or gas to migrate from underneath neighboring property)
## Support Rights
Natural support provided to piece of land by surrounding land
Subjacent Support - Provided by the underlying earth.
Lateral Support - Provided by neighboring land.
I.E. If you and Fred own adjacent lots, if Fred excavates his foundation for a build and results in part of your lot collapsing -> Fred may be required to pay you compensation 
## Attachments
Natural Attachments - Things attached ot the earth by roots, such as trees and shrubs. (In some cases, crops to be harvested are treated as personal property)
Doctrine of Emblements - Special legal rule pertaining to crops. Applies when agricultural land has been leased to a tenant farmer. Emblements are crops planted annually by a tenant. If lease terminates (through no fault of the tenant) before a crop is mature, the tenant has the right to re-enter the land later to harvest the first crop that matures after the tenancy terminates.
Severance - Natural attachments can be sold separately from the land, this is sometimes referred to as severance from the real property. (i.e. selling your standing timber to a logging company - once sold, it is treated as the buyer's personal property even before it is cut down)
Fixtures - Man-made attachments, they are items that have been attached to real property in such a way that they have become part of it. (i.e. houses, fences...)
- Distinguishing Fixtures from Personal Property - Written agreement, buyer and seller can make any mutually acceptable agreement concerning a particular item, regardless of whether it would usually be considered an attachment or fixture, or the seller's personal property
## Fixture Tests
Sometimes, there's no written agreement concerning an item that the buyer and the seller each claim should be his, to decide, courts have developed certain test used to classify as either real property or personal property.
Method of Attachment - First test is whether or not an item is a fixture. Is the item attached to the realty in some way, and if so, how? The less movable an item is, the more likely it is to be considered to be a fixture. (i.e. bolts, plaster, cement, growing plants). Also a fixture if permanently resting on the land on a foundation (gazebo).
- Constructively annexed - items considerd part of real property even if it's movable because it's so strongly connected with property (i.e. specialized tools required for machinery in a factory, keys to house, free standing statue part of a design of fish pond)
Adaptation Test - Whether the item has been specially adapted to the realty in some way, or was specially designed for it.
Intention Test - Original intention of the person who installed the item that's now in dispute. (judge will look for evidence indicating whether the item was installed with the intention of making it a permanent part of the structure) (most important)
Relationship Test - Another way of establishing the intention of the person who installed the item, or arranged to have it installed. Person's relationship to real property at the time of installation. (did he own the property or was he a tenant?)
- Assumed that tenant intend to take their belonging with them when they move, even if the items were attached to the realty during the tenancy.
## Trade Fixtures
Equipments and other items that a commercial tenant installs to carry on a business. Even if they are call fixtures, they aren't considered part of the real property, no matter how they are attached. Unless there's some written agreement, trade fixtures may always be removed by the business operator at end of tenancy -> after removing  trade fixture, tenant may be required to restore property to its original condition or compensate landlord for any damage.
## Manufactured Homes
Homes assembled at a factory and transported to the dwelling site rather than constructed on the site. Initially personal property and subject to registration similar to motor vehicles. If home owner also owns the land on which the home is affixed, owner may go through title elimination (this eliminated the vehicle title, making the home a fixture and part of the real property).
- Mobile home may later be removed from the real property and sold again as personal property. Mobile home's new owner may restore vehicle title; it may also remain as real property if it is installed immediately on another parcel.
## Methods of Land Description
Legal Description - Description that enables a piece of land to be precisely identified.
Metes and Bounds - Oldest method still in use, metes are measurements and bounds are boundaries. Parcel of land is described in terms of its boundary lines. Elements use to define the location of each boundary are monuments, courses, and distances.
- Monument marks a fixed point, it may be a natural object or it may be man-made such as a survey marker or centerline of a road.
- Course is a compass direction such as 'northwest'. Indicates the direction in which each boundary line runs.
- Stated in terms of the degree of deviation from either north or south, whichever is closer. 
- Mush give distance for each boundary, stating how far it extends. 
- Point of beginning must always be described by referene to monument, although it doesn't have to be the monument itself.
- Must give directions including course and distance starting from beginning and making a circuit back to start.
Government Survey - Property is identified by its location in a particular section, township, and range on a U.S. government survey map. Government survey system (also called rectangular survey system) is made up of series of very large sruvey grids covering much of U.S.
- Each of the grids has its own principal meridian (the main north-south line in the grid) and its own base line (main east-west line).
- Every principal meridian has a name and each survey grids in the system is identified by the name of its principal meridian. (i.e. Willamette Meridian for the survey description of land in WA and OR)
- On each grid, gridlines run parallel to principal meridian and to base line at intervals of six miles (called Range Lines). They divide the land into columns called Ranges.
- East-west lines that parallel base line called Township Lines, they divide the land into rows called Township Tiers.
- Individual square (where township tier intersects range) is called township. A township measures six miles by six miles. Particular township identified by its position in relation to the base line and principal meridian.
- Each township divided into 36 sections, each section is one mile on each side (each section is 640 acres). Sections within township numbered from 1 to 36, numbering start in upper right-hand corner, moves left to section 6, then drops down a row and moves right to section 12, and so on, snaking back and forth until lower right-hand corner of township is reached.
- In most cases, particular owner's property is only part of a section, so it's described in terms of fractions, such as quarter sections or quarter-quarter sections, or even smaller fractions.
- Best way to read government survey description is to start at end with section number, and move backwards to the quarter section. Then move to each progressively smaller piece of land. A complete government survey description for a particular piece of property must include the section, township, and range. It must also include the name of the principal meridian to identify grid.
- Government Lots - Irregularly shaped sections, along with sections that aren't exactly one mile square for other reasons, are each assigned a special number.
Lot and Block - Standard method fro describing property in towns and cities.
- When developers subdivide land for housing, they divide into lots, with single house built on each lot. Each lot is assigned lot number and groups of lots separated by streets within the subdivision may be assigned block numbers.
- Plat Map - Map showing lots and blocks in subdivision. Property described by this method is often called platted property. Before starting to sell individual lots, developers record the plat map in county where subdivision is located.
- Boundaries of entire parcel of land, and of each individual lot within it, should be described using either metes and bounds method or government survey method on plat map. Once plat map has been recorded, a lot within the subdivisions can be described by its lot number and block number, along with name of subdivision and the county or city in which it is located. 
- Air Lots - Airspace occupied by a unit sometimes referred to as air lot. Descriptions of this type make reference to an established plane of elevation called datum.
## Estates in Land
Interest - Right concerning the property or a claim against it.
Possessory Interests - Includes the right to exclusive use and possession of property. (Also called estates)
Estate - Right to possess and have exclusive use of real property, either now or in the future.
Lien (such as mortgage) - Interest in propeerty (but not estate), it's a nonpossessory interest. (Lender who holds the lien doesn't have the right to take possession of the property)
Two basic categories of estates: freehold estates and leasehold estates. Both include exclusive right of possession, freehold estates include title; leasehold estate do not.
- Freehold Estates - Two main kinds of freehold estates: fee simple estates and life estates.
   - Fee Simple Estates - Most common type of estate (also called the fee estate or estate in fee). Are either absolute or defeasible. Highest and most complete form of land ownership (greatest estate in real property that person can have). Can potentially last forever, owner can transfer the estate or any portion of the estate in any manner they wish (perpetual, transferrable, and inheritable).
      - Fee Simple Absolute - Owner transfers title to new owner with no special limitations, and the new owner is free to do whatever he or she wishes with the property.
      - Defeasible Fee Estate (Qualified fee estate or Conditional fee estate) - States that new owner will own property until or unless a specifice act or event takes place. (For when owners want to control future use of land after selling)
      - Owner of defeasible fee estate hold same interest as owner of a fee simple absolute, except that the defeasible fee owner's interest will end if the specified act or event occurs at some later date.
      - Defeasible fee estate can be classified either as simple determinable or as fee simple subject to condition subsequent
      - Fee Simple Determinable - If act or event prohibitied in deed occurs, title automatically reverts to person who imposed limitation, or to their heirs (known as possibility of reverter).
      - Fee Simple Subject to Condition Subsequent - If prohibited act or event occurs, person who imposed limitation (or heirs) will regain title only if they take action to do so.
      - If deed does not specify whether a fee simple interest is absolute or defeasible, it is simply assumed to be absolute.
  - Life Estates - Limited in time, lasts only as long as particular person is alive (life tenant). 
     - Measuring Life - Life upon which life estate depends. If measuring life is someone other than life tenant, it is called pur autre vie.
     - Life tenant can sell, lease, or mortgage his interest, but that interest is only a life estate.
     - Future Interest - Ownership interest that will begin when life estate ends. (Either estate in reversion if future interest held by grantor/heir or estate in remainder if held by someone else).
     - Duties of a Life Tenant - 1. Must pay taxes and other liens in timely manner. 2. Must allow reversioners or remaindermen to inspect property. 3. Must not commit waste. (Must not injure, alter, or destroy premises, and must keep the property in good repair).
- Leasehold Estate - For limited time, tenant has right to exclusive possession and use of property (landlord called lessor and tenant called lessee). Chattel real is personal property closely tied to real estate. Most leasehold estates can be inherited by tenant's heirs.
   - Estate for Years - Leasehold estate that lasts for any fixed term (may be any specified number of years, months, weeks, or days). Also called term tenancy.
      - No notices is required when lease ends within said dates.
      - If one side wanted to terminate sooner, you may do so only if one party has breached the lease, or if you both agree to end the lease. Termination by mutual consent is called surrender.
   - Periodic Estate - When tenancy is not limited to a specific term. Most are month-to-month, but they can be week-to-week. Continues from rent period to rent period, until one of the parties gives the other party notice of termination. Otherwise, lease will be automatically renewed for additional rental period.
      - ** Under new Washington law, landlords cannot end periodic tenancy simply by giving notice. Residential tenants under month-to-month lease can stay on indefinitely unless the landlord has "just cause" for eviction. Just cause for eviction exists if tenant significantly breaches lease agreement, or if the owner takes the unit off the market (to occupy it herself or to substantially remodel or demolish it).
  - Estate at Will - No specified lease term and can be terminated at any time by either landlord or tenant (although residential landlord may need just cause to terminate). Rent may or may not be paid. Typical cases would be where tenant provided maintenance services to landlord instead of paying rent
     - Cannot be assigned to someone else. Estate at will automatically ends upon death of either party.
 - Estate at Sufferance - Not actually true estate. When tenant comes into possession of property under valid lease, but holds over after tenancy has expired, staying on against landlord's wishes. Sometimes called holdover tenant. No termination required as tenant no longer has legal possession of property.
## Ways of Holding Title
Ownership in Severalty - Ownership by one individual (separate or sole ownership).
- Can be owned in severalty either by natural person or by artificial person (legal entity such as business, city, or state government).
Concurrent Ownership - When two or more persons have ownership interests in a property at the same time (co-owners or concurrent owners).
- ** Washington: Tenancy in Common - Default category, if owners of property did not specifically name another type of co-ownership, they are tenants in common.
   - Two or more persons must each have an ownership interest in a single piece of property. Their shares don't have to be equal while that is common.
   - Interests held by tenants in common (and other types) are called undivided interests, they apply to the property as a whole, not just a portion of it.
   - Tenant in common is free to sell, will, or mortgage his or her interest without the consent of other tenants.
   - Tenancy in common will terminate if all of co-tenants sell their interests to single owner, or co-tenants may agree to voluntary partition. To partition a property is to divide it into separate properties that are each owned by a co-tenant in severalty. If tenants can't agree on what to do with property, one of the co-tenants may file legal action (suit for partition) asking judge to divide property up among them. If it won't to subdivide the land itself, judge will order property to be sold. 
- ** Washington: Joint Tenancy - Two or more persons are join and equal owners of a property. 
   - **Right of Surviviorship** - When join tenant dies, his or her interest in property automatically passes to surviving joint tenant(s). 
   - For join tenancy to be created, or for existing join tenancy to continue, law requires fulfillment of four conditions know as the four unities: 
      - Unity of Interest - Each join tenant must hold same estate. Each must have equal interest in property
      - Unity of Title - Join tenants must receive their intersts in the property through same deed or will.
      - Unity of Time - Joint tenants must all receive their interests in the property at the same time. 
      - Unity of Possession - Joint tenants have the right to possess and use the entire property, not just a particular portion of it.
      - If any of the four unities don't exist at the beginning, or is broken, then joint tenancy isn't established in the first place, or it is destroyed - either partially or entirely. 
      - Because of the Right of Survivorship, property owned in join tenancy isn't considered part of deceased joint tenant's estate, thus joint tenancy interest can't be willed. Since joint tenancy isn't part of deceased's property, it doesn't have to go through probate process. Any liens or creditor's claims against deceased joint tenant's interests are extinguished at death. 
- ** Washington: Community Property - Property owned concurrently and equally by two spouses. Everything owned by a married couple that is not the separate property of one spouse is the community property of both spouses. There is legal presumption that all property acquired by spouse during marriage is community property. Property that spouse acquired through skill or labor during marriage, or purchased with community funds or community credit. 
   - Separate Property - All property owned by one spouse before marriage is considered separate property. Anything purchased with separate funds is his or her separate property. Profites or proceeds from separate property, such as appreciation, rents, or interest from separate property, are also classified as a spouse's separate property. 
   - Each spouse has undivided 50% interest in all of the couple's community property.
   - Joinder Requirement - Signatures of both spouses are required to list, sell, or to encumber community real property, or to purchase real property that will be community property. Transfer of community personal property does not require other spouse's consent, except for certain items, such as household furnishings. Best to have seller's spouse sign transaction documents even if seller believes property is separate property. 
## Business Organizations
Business Entity - Owned by one or more individuals, but separated from those individuals. Law considers it to be an artificial person that can enter into contracts and own property. Has same property rights as any other person. Its perpetual existenance prevents an artficial person from owning property in join tenancy. When corporation or similar business entity owns property with another, they take title as tenants in common. 
Partnership - Association of two or more persons to carry on a business as co-owners and divide the profits. 
- General Partnership - Each partner has ownership interest in partnership, and a voice in management of business. Each partner has right to share in its profits, and obligation to share in its losses. Partners' ownership interests may be equal or unequal, depending on terms of contractual agreement. Partnership agreement isn't required to be in writing, but is a good idea. 
   - Property is considered to be partnership property if it's acquired in the partnership's name. Also partnership property if acquired in the name of one or more of the individual partners and the deed makes reference to partnership. 
   - Unless otherwise agreed, all of the partners have equal right to use and possess the partnership property for partnership purposes. But partner is not co-owner of the partnership property and has no transferable interest in it. If partnership is owned equally by three individuals, each would have equal right to use and possess partnership-owned property, but it is parternship (business entity) that owns it. 
   - But partner's interest in the partnership may be transferred. 
   - Each partner may be held personally liable for the debts and obligations of ther general partnership. If creditors cannot satisfy their claims out of the partnership property, they can demand payment out of and individual partner's own property. 
- Limited Partnership - More structured and regulated than general partnerships. Can be created only by filing a certificate of limited partnership with the office of the secretary of state. Limited partnership agreement must be in writing. 
   - Has at least one general partner, plus one or more limited partners. Limited partners have limited liability, which means that they are not personally liable for partnership's debts, only general partners are responsible. 
   - Uniform Limited Partnership Act originally allowed only the general partners to manage or control the business. Limited partners could lose limited liability if they participated in management. Now, act allows limited partners full participation without affecting their limited liability. 
- Corporations - Make up of shareholders, board of directors, and corporate officers. 
   - Shareholders (stockholders) - Investors who own shared in corporation.
   - Shareholders elect board of directors, which has overall control over the business.
   - Board supervises officers of the coporation, who manage the business from day to day. 
   - Corporate property is owned in severalty by the corporation, not by the shareholders. 
   - Securities - Shares in a corporation. Financial investment in a company made by a investor who does not have managerial control over the business (other investments, such as interest in limited partnership, may also be classified as securities).
   - Blue Sky Laws - State laws that regulate securities. Securities may only be sold by licensed dealer. 
   - Corporation itself is liable for its debts and for illegal actions taken in its name. Sharesholders not personally liable for corporation's debts or actions. 
- Limited Liability Companies (LLC) - Combines many of advantages of partnership with many of the advantages of corporation. All of LLC's owners (referred as members) may manage the company, or certain members may be appointed to manage it. Unlike general partners, LLC's managing members aren't personally liable for th LLC's debts, All LLC members have limited liability, like coporate shareholders.
   - Corporations have problem of double taxation. Corporate earnings are first taxed as corporate income, then, when the earnings are distributed to stockholders, they are taxed again, as the personal income of the stockholders. By contract, earnings of LLC are taxed only as the personal income of members. 
- Joint Ventures - Not intended to be an ongoing business endeavor. Two or more individuals or organizations join together for one specific project. There are no formal requirements for the creation of it. Parties simply agree that they're going to work together on a particular project, and then they share profits or losses. 
- Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) - Business association that invests almost exclusively in real estate or eal estate financing. It qualifies for special tax treatment - as long as trust distributes at least 90% of its income to shareholder, it can avoid double taxation. Trust passes profits through to its investors and pays taxes only on the earnings it retains. 
   - To qualify for this tax advantage, REIT must meet strict requirements. It must have large number of investors (at least 100) and derive at least 75% of its income from real estate. 
   - Interests in a real estate investment trust are freely transferrable. Shares are securities, and their sale is subject to federal securities laws and the supervision of the SEC. 
   - REIT may be set up as a trust. In a trust, trustees invest and manage the trust property on behalf of trust beneficiaries. Trustees are required to follow guidelines in documents that established the trust. 
## Co-ownership Properties
Condominimus - Although they can be commercial or industrial, most condominiums are residential. Typically made up of one or more multi-family residential buildings and looks just like an apartment complex. Instead of renting a dwelling unit, each condominium resident owns a unit in severalty, in fee simple. All of the unit owners share ownership of the condominimum's common elements as tenants in common. Major decisions are made by members of condominium association (or homeowners association). All unit owners belong to the association. 
- Common Elements - Parts of the condominium thaty may be used by all of the residents (common areas). Specific undivided interest in the common elements is assigned to each unit. 
- Condominium Declaration - Document prepared and recorded by developer that provides detailed information about every aspect of condominium property. 
- Actual boundaries of condominium unit are finished interior surfaces of its walls, floors, and ceilings (anything within those boundaries ordinarily owned by unit owner). All aspects of condominium property outside of units are common elements. 
- Limited Common Elements - Owned by all of unit owners as tenants in common. But reserved for exclusive use of owners of a specific unit or specific group of units (i.e. assigned parking space). 
- Individual units are separate properties - buyer receives fee simple deed for the unit and separate title insurance policy. 
Conversion - Changing of apartment complex into condominium. Subject ot state regulations that are designed to protect rights of tenants of apartment building. 
Cooperatives - Title of cooperative project held by corporation formed for that purpose. Corporation owns property, residents simply own shares in the corporation. Sharesholder do not actually own the units. Each shareholder has long-term proprietary lease on unit. Interest in cooperative is not title to real estate. 
- Financed with single mortgage, and also taxed as single property. 
- Rent that shareholder-tenant pays to corporation is proportionate share of cooperative's operating expenses. One tenant's failure to pay her share can affect all of other tenants. 
Timeshares - Instead of purchasing condominium unit outright, timeshare buyer purchases right to occupy unit during a particular time slot each year (usually in resort areas). 
- Washington Timeshare Act - Consumer protection law governing sale of timeshares. Requires timeshare developments to be registered with Department of Licensing. People involved in selling timeshares must be registered as timeshare salespersons. 
   - Under the act, prospective buyers must be given disclosure statement. Also gives buyers seven-day right of recission. Buyers may cancel their purchase and sale agreement within seven days after signing agreement or receiving disclosure statement, whichever occurs later. 
   - Real estate licensee can handle timeshare resales without registering under act as timeshare salesperson, provided that licensee: 1. is acting only in brokerage capacity. 2. is not selling inventory owned by the licensee or her firm 3. complies with other requirements of the act, including giving buyers disclosure statement and seven-day right of recission. 
## Transfer of Real Property
Culmination of real estate transaction
Alienation - All of different methods by which title to real property can be transferred from one person to another. Transfer of ownership is form of alienation. But, it is the opposite of acquisition of property. 
- Can be volunatry or involuntary. Some cases, title can be transferred without action by owners, and even against owner's will (foreclosure). 
- Voluntary Alienation - Transferring of property by patent, deed, will, or dedication.
Patent - Transfer of property into private hands from government. 
Deeds - Document private landowner uses to tranfer real property to someone else, usually prepared by lender or title company in residential transaction. Property owner is called grantor, person owner is transferring property to is called grantee. Transfer of property by means of deed is referred to as conveyance. 
- Warranty Deed - Most common type (general warranty deed). Grantor gives grantee specific guarantees, or warranties, about status of title to property. These warranties are known as covenants. Warranty deed carries five basic convenants, these apply automatically whenever warranty deed is used, they do not have to be set fourth in deed itself: 1. Seisin - grantor promises that she actually owns interest that deed is conveying to grantee. 2. Right to Convey - grantor promises she has right to convey interest in question to grantee. 3. Against Encumbrances - grantor promises that there are no easements, liens, or other encumbrances against title that have not been disclosed to grantee. 4. Quiet Enjoyment - promise that grantee's possession of property will not be disturbed by lawful claims of any third parties. 5. Warranty - grantor promises to defend grantee's title against any claims by third parties that existed at time of conveyance was made.
   - Because covenants are not limited to grantor's period of ownership, warranty deed provided greater protection to grantee thatn any other type of deed (while creating greatest liability for grantor). 
- Special Warranty Deed - Carries same convenats as warranty deed, buth wil limited scope. Convenants only cover problems that arose while grantor owned property. Use cases could be if grantor is fiduciary (i.e. executor of estate) who is holding land only temporarily). 
- After-acquired Title - both warranty and special warranty deeds convey this to guarantee. It is interest in property that the grantor acquires after executing deed. 
- Quitclaim Deed - Unliked warranty deed, it does not convey after-acquired title. Conveys only interest in property that grantor has at time deed is executed. If grantor has no interest in property at that point, quitclaim deed conveys nothing to grantee. Also has no warranties or covenants. Common use case is for clearing up minor title defects, called cloud on title. Clouds are problems appearing in publid record that cast doubt on status of title. 
   - Reformation deed - when quitclaim deed is used to cure cloud on title. 
   - Also used involving co-ownership , when one co-owner is conveying interest in property to other co-owner. 
   - Not appropriate for standard real estate transaction, buyer should insist on warranty deed. If seller tries to use quitclaim deed, that should be red flag as there may be problems with title. 
- Miscellaneous Deeds - 1. Sheriff's Deed - convey title to someone who purchaes property at a mortgage foreclosure sale. 2. Trustee Deed - used to convey title to purchaser at trustee's sale when deed of trust is foreclosed. 3. Tax Deed - when government forecloses on property because property taxes weren't paid, foreclosure sale purchaser receives it. 4. Gift Deed - if one party givers property to another. Gift deed not supported by consideration, and is easiest for court to set aside if there is any hint it was executed in order to defraud creditors. 
## Transfer by Deed
Six requirements apply for transfer of any deed: deed must be in writing, identify the parties, be signed by competent grantor, specify living grantee, contain words of conveyance, and adequately describe property. 
In Writing - Deed must always be in writing. Statue of frauds is state law requiring certain types of contracts and transactions to be put into writing, under statue, oral conveyance of title to real property is not valid. 
Identify the Parties - It isn't absolutely necessary to state grantee's name in the deed, as long as it includes a description that makes it possible to identify her. (i.e. deed could be valid if grantor conveyd title "to my sister," as long as grantor had only one sister). 
Signed by Competent Grantor - deed without grantor's signature is ineffecitve, even if it has all of other elements needed for validity. Also, deed is valid only if grantor is legally competent when he signs it (must be at least 18 years old in Washington and of sound mind). 
Living Grantee - Isn't necessary for grantee to be legally competent, but grantee must be alive at time of conveyance. However, grantee's signature isn't necessary, in fact, grantee ordinarily doesn't sign the deed. 
Words of Conveyance - Valid deed must include words of conveyance, sometimes called granting clause. Deed must explicitly state in some way that the property is being transferred from grantor to grantee. 
Property Description - Vaid deed must have an adequate property description. Although complete legal description isn't required, deed must make clear what property is being conveyed. Description is adequate if surveyor could us it to locate the property. 
Grantor's Signature - If grantor can't sign deed himself, it may be signed by grantor's attorney in fact. Attorney in fact is not necessarily a lawyer, but is someone grantor has appointed as his representative. Document called power of attorney is used to appoint attorney in fact. 
All Owners Must Sign - when property being transferred is owned by more than one person, all of owners must sign deed as grantors. If previous deed named several grantees, all of those grantees should be signing new deed as grantors. 
Community Property or Homestead - To convey community property, both spouses must sign deed. If grantor is married, his or her spouse should also sign, even if they don't think property is community property. 
Other Requirements - To carry out conveyance, along with signing valid deed, additional steps need to be carried out. 
- Acknowledgment - Signing legal document in presence of formal witness, ordinarily a notary public. Witness should have no interest in property being conveyed. Grantor gives notary public proof of identity and signs statement declaring that he is sigining deed voluntarily. Notary also signs statement declaring she witnessed acknowledgement of the document. 
   - Valid deed will transfer title from grantor to grantee even if it not acknowledged. But in Washington, a deed cannot be recorded unless it has been acknowledged. 
- Delivery and Acceptance - Even when deed complies with all of rules for validity, transfer only becomes effective when grantor has delivered the deed to grantee, and grantee has accepted it. Acceptance of deed completes transfer of title. 
   - Washington recently began allowing "transfer on death deeds", this type of deed transfers real property to grantee automatically (without probate) when grantor dies. Deed must state that transfer will take place on grantor's death, also deed must be recorded. 
- Additional Deed Elements - Most deeds have additional features that aren't absolutely necessary, though typically included. 
   - Habendum Clause - Defines/limits grantee's estate. May specify fee simple ownership or some lesser interest, such as life estate. (To have and to hold clause). If warranty deed doesn't have habendum clause, it is presumed to convey fee simple absolute. 
   - Exclusions and Reservations Clause - Most deeds contain brief statement concerning encumbrances. Lists any easements, private restrictions, or liens that will burden grantee's title. Valid encumbrances ordinarily continue in effect  even if they aren't listed in deed. 
   - Recital of Consideration - In most transactions, consideration for the conveyance is the purchase money that grantee gave grantor in exchange for property. Consideration exchanged in transaction doesn't have to be stated in deed for document to be valid. But, recital of consideration appears in many deeds (can state any amount other than purchase price i.e "for 1 dollar and other..."). Purpose is to indicate that property wasn't gift from grantor to grantee. If property were a gift, grantor's creditors might still be able to assert their claims against it. 
   - Date of Execution - Deed can be valid even though it's undated. But as matter of convenience, it's important to include date of conveyance on deed. 
## Other Methods of Voluntary Alienation
Will is another type of document that may be used to transfer property ownership voluntarily. (deed used to transfer property when property owner is alive, will used upon his death). 
Wills - Must meet three requirements to be valid: must be in writing, signed by testator, and attested to by two or more competent witnesses. (testator is person making will, witnesses sign statement indicating that testator signed document in their presence and declared it to be his will.)
- Normally, testator appoints executor in the will, it is executor's responsibility to carry out terms of the will after testator's death. Legal process of establishing validity of will and distributing property according to its terms i called probate. 
- If no executor was named in the will, court will appoint a person to manage and distribute estate of the deceased (called administrator). 
- May be used to transfer testator's entire estate, inclduing both real property and personal property. Testator who transfers personal property by will is said to bequeath the property, recipient is called legatee. When real property is transferred by will, testator is said to devise the property, recipient is called devisee.
- While a will usually must be in writing, in limited situations a spoken will is valid. Testator must be on her deathbed when stating the terms of the will, and there must be at least two witnesses present (called nuncupative will). In Washington, nuncupative will can transfer no more than $1,000 worth of personal property. Real property can never be transferred by a nuncupative well. 
- Holographic Will - Unwitnessed will dated and written entirely in testator's own handwriting, rather thatn typewritten or filled in on a printed for. While some states recognize holographic wills, they are not **valid*** in Washington. 
Dedication - Transfer of real estate from private ownership to public ownership, without payment to the private owner. In some cases, dedication is involuntary, but it can be voluntary gift to the public. 
## Involuntary Alientation by Rule of Law
Can occur by rule of law, or through adverse possession, or as result of natural forces.
Involuntary Dedication - Dedication made under some legal constraint. 
- Statutory Dedication - To obtain permission to subdivide land, developer is required to dedicate some land in the subdivision for public streets and utility easements. 
- Common Law Dedication - If property owner has allowed public use of his property for along period of time, such as allowing people to cross his property to reach lakeshore. Local government may consider property to have been dedicated. 
Intestate Succession - Person who dies without leaving valid will is said to die intestate (person who dies with will dies testate). Title to property passes to owner's nearest living relatives according to rules of intestate succession (set forth in state statute). 
- Those who are entitled to property through intestate succession called heirs. 
- Those who acquire property by intestate succession receive by descent, rather than by devise or bequest. 
- Probate court will appoint an administrator to distribute intestate person's real and personal property to the heirs. 
Escheat - When person dies intestate and probate court is unable to locate any heirs, ownership of the property reverts to the state. State may also acquire ownership of abandoned property through escheat. 
Condemnation - If government entity decides piece of property is needed for public use, it has constitutionial power to buy the property from owner, even if owner doesn't want to sell. This is called eminent domain, and legal procedure used to exercise that power is called condemnation. Forced sale in this context is called government taking or just taking. 
- U.S. Constitution sets two basic requirements for condemnation: 1. Government's intended use of the property must be public use. 2. Government must pay the private owner just compensation. 
Alienation by Court Order - Property can be involuntarily transferred by court order. 
- Quiet Title Action - Cloud on title generally makes a property unmarketable. If partices can't agree on quitclaim deed, either party may start a quiet title action. In quiet title action, court decides who holds what interest in property. 
- Partition Suit - Way of dividing up property owned by more than one person. If co-owners can't agree on how to divide the property, or if they disagree about whether to sell it, one or more of the co-owners can file a suit for partition. Judge will decide how to divide property, and this decision will be binding on all the co-owners. 
   - If phsyical division of property isn't feasible, judge will order property to be sold and divide proceeds among the co-owners. 
- Foreclosure - Creditor with lien against a debtor's property has right to foreclose on property if debt isn't paid. To foreclose, creditor files a lawsuit asking court to order property to be sold, so that the debt can be paid out of the sale proceeds. 
## Adverse Possession and Natural Forces
In adverse possession, someone acquires legal title to another person's property by occupying it over a long period without the owner's permission. 
Adverse Possession - Encourages fullest and most productive use of land. Title is transferred from owner who has ignored property for long period of time to someone who is actively putting it to use. Almost any real property is subject to adverse possession except for property owned by government. There are five basic requirements for adverse possession, possession must be: acutal, open and notorious, hostile to owner's interest, exclusive, and continuous without interruption. 
- Actual Possession - Occupation and use of property in appropriate manner. For residential property, actual possession would mean residing on property, for agricultrual property, actual possession would mean putting land to agricultural use. 
- Open and Notorious - If enough to put average owner on notice that someone else is making use of her property. Adverse possessor's use must be conspicuous, not hidden. This ensures that the true owner will have reasonable chance to prevent adverse possessino by ejecting adverse possessor from property. 
- Hostile - Adverse possessor essentially must act as if he owns the property. As general rule, possession is considered hostile as long as adverse possessor does not have true owner's permission to be there. Possession with owner's permission can never lead to transfer of title. 
   - Possession under color of title is considered hostile. Adverse possessor has color of title if she mistakenly believes that property belongs to her, because a defective deed or other document says that it's hers. (Some cases, color of title may shorten time period needed for adverse possession). 
- Exclusive - If true owner is still using the property, it can't be adversely possessed. 
- Continuous and Uninterrupted - Required period is usually ten years in Washington (it is reduced to seven years if adverse possessor has color of title and pays property taxes on parcel). 
   - Tacking - Some cases, successive periods of continuous and uninterrupted possession by series of adverse possessors add up to required statutory period. 
- When all requirements are fulfilled, title passes from original owner to adverse possessor automatically. Some cases, neither party even realizes that this has happened. 
   - Because there's no documented evidence that title ahs changed hands, adverse possessor's title won't be marketable until he perfects it. He can either record quitclaim deed from former owner or file quite title suit. 
Natural Forces - Real property can be involuntarily transferred by natural forces. 
- Accretion - Land beside body of water is increased by accretion when waterbone silt is deposited on shore (deposits called alluvion or alluvium). Accretion occurs gradually so build-up of alluvion is almost imperceptible. When land is added to by accretion, landowner acquires title to newly deposited soil. 
- Reliction - When body of water recedes slowly, exposing land that's been underwater. Adjoining landowner acquires title to newly exposed land (also gradual process). 
- Avulsion - Occurs due to sudden change in watercourse, often as result of storm and flooding. This usually does not result in transfer of title. Can also refere to when land is violently torn away by flowing water. If it's possible to so, owner may reclaim land within a reasonable period. If it isn't reclaimed, it becomes part of the property where it ended up. 
## The Recording System
Recording a document means having it placed in public record, so that the information set forth in the document is available to the general public. Deed is still valid even if it is not recorded. However, if there is a dispute over ownership, the holder of an unrecorded deed can lose possession to a subsequent purchase who did record his deed. 
Procedures - To have document recorded, you file it at county records office of where the property in question is located. State law prescribes procedures that county must use in recording documents. When document is filed for recording, data and time of filing are noted, and document is assigned recording number. This establishes document's priority, in case there are competiting claims. After document has been scanned, original will be returned to appropriate party. 
- Records search will determine validity of seller's title and reveal any recorded claims against the property. Records may be searched by grantor or grantee name, or by various other identifiers such as property's tax parcel number. 
Title Searching - Researching who holds what interest in a piece of property (usually performed when property is about to be sold). Can look up seller's name to make sure seller hasn't already conveyed the property to someone else. Can also find deed or other document by which selled acquired title. Tracing chain of title back through several owners establishes validity of seller's title. 
Notice - Primary purpose of recording deed is to provide public notice of grantee's interest in the property. Notice is an important legal concept, legal rights and liabilities often depend on whether a party had notice of a particular fact at a given time. 
- Actual Notice - If person is aware of it (she has observed it herself, or someone else has informed her of it).
- Cosntructive Notice - If person should be aware of it, even if she's not (information is available and could be obtained by taking reaonable steps to do so). 
- Anyone who later acquires an interest in property is considered to have constructive notice of earlier recorded document, even if he didn't have actual notice of it. 
- As general rule, the one who recorded his or her deed first would have good title. 
- Like recording, possession of property also provides notice of an interest in the property or a claim against it. 
- Almost any document affect title to real property may be recorded, and most should be. Documents transferring title, establishing an interest in property, or creating or removing encumbrances should be recorded. (lis pendens is a document which state that a pending lawsuit may affect title to a given property).
- One common document that is not recorded is purchase and sale agreement (sales contract). 
## Title Insurance
A title insurance company not only investigates chain of title, it offers buyer additional protection of title insurance. 
In some states, buyers obtain only an abstract of title, which is examined by an attorney who then delivers an opinion concerning condition of title. However, abstract of title is merely a history of recorded interests in the property, it does not offer financial protections against undiscovered defects that is provded by title insurance. 
Title Insurance Policy - Contract between title insurance company and policyholder (normally property buyer or lender providing financing for a transaction). 
- Owner's Policy - Issued to buyer.
- Mortgagee's Policy - Protects lender's security interest. 
- Title insurance company agrees to indemnify the policyholder, meaning it will reimburse policyholder for financial losses resulting from title defects covered by the policy. Title company will also handle legal defense if someone makes claim against the title. 
Obtaining a Policy - First step in obtaining title insurance for a transaction is title search. Buyer or seller pays a fee to title company to cover cost of searching public record for information affecting the property's title. For certain types of coverage, property will also be inspected for problems that don't appear in the public record, and a survey may be performed. Based on its investigation, company issues title report for the property. All defects and encumbrances found in public record or during an inspection or survey are listed in the report. These will be excluded from coverage when policy is issued. 
- Company charges a one-time premium and policy lasts as long as the policyholder has a legal interest in the property. 
- Key point is that title insurance policy overs problems that were not discovered in title search process (rather than the problems that were discovered and were therefore listed as exceptions to coverage). Policy provides protection against problems that were unknown at the time policy was issued but may come to light later on. 
- Standard Coverage - Protects against problems concerning matters of public record. Insures against losses resulting from any encumbrances of record that the title searcher overlooked and failed to include in the title report. Also insures against hidden problems with recorded documents, such as forged signatures, fraud, incompetency, and improperly delivered deeds. But it does not insure against claims and problems that might be disclosed by an inspection or survey of the property, such as adverse possession or an encroachment. 
- Extended Coverage - Covers same problems as stnadard coverage, plus problems that might have been discovered through inspection of the property, but were not. Title company sends inspector or surveyor to property to look for encroachments and other potential probmems. Problems discovered in the inspection or survey will be listed in the policy and excluded from coverage but problems missed will be covered. Whenever buyer finances real estate purchase with institutional loan, lender will requst extended coverage policy to protect its interest. (Mortgagee's policy is nearly always an extended coverage policy). 
- Homeowner's Coverage - Availble in sales of residential property with up to four units (policy most home buyers require). Covers most of the same title problems an extended coverage policy does. However, instead of protecting the lender, homeowner's policy protects the buyer. 
- No Protection Against Government Action - Does not insure against losses due to government action, such as condemnation or zoning changes. 
## Encumbrances
Nonpossessionry interests in real property (doesn't include ownership or possession of property). Doesn't have right to exclusive possession life freehold or leasehold estate. 
Financial - Liens (ie mortgage)
Nonfinancial - Easements and private restrictions
Liens - Person who holds lien has financial claim against property owner. Creditor's security interest in owner's property, makes property security (or collateral) for the debt and force a foreclosure of property. 
- Secured Creditor - Creditor who has lien against one or more pieces of debtor's property.
- Without lien, if debt isn't paid, unsecured creditor has only right to sue debtor. 
- Lien doesn't prevent debtor from selling property, but new owner will take property subject to the lien (creditor can still foreclose if debt isn't paid). 
- General lien attaches to all of debtor's property (i.e. judgement lien (involuntary lien))
- Specific lien is one that attaches only to specific piece of property (i.e. mortgage (voluntary lien)). 
- Mortgage - Lien created by contract between property owner and lender. Owner gives lender mortgage as security for repayment of the loan, so that property serves as collateral. 
   - Owner is mortgagor and lender is mortgagee. 
- Deed of Trust - Borrower is trustor/grantor, lender is beneficiary, and neutral third party called trustee, who handles foreclosure if necessary. 
- Construction Liens (involuntary) - Claimed against real property by anyone who provides labor, materials, or professional services fo improvement of property. Attaches only to property where lien claimant performed construction work or supplied materials. (Also called mechanic's lien / materialman's lien). 
   - Claimant must record claim of lien no later than 90 days after claimant has stopped working on or providing materials for the project. Even when construction lien is recorded in time, it will expire if lienholder doesn't start foreclosure proceedings within eight months after recording the claim. 
- Judgement Liens (involuntary) - If court determines that one party in lawsuit owes money to other, judgement is entered against losing party. Winner is judgement creditor and loser is judgement debtor. It is general lien and attaches to all property owned by judgement debtor. If judgement debtor owns property in county outside one where lawsuit took place, judgement creditor can record abstract of judgement in that county to obtain lien agains all of that property. 
   - Lasts until it is paid, or until it expires. In Washingotn, lien expires ten years after judgement was entered. During lien period, if debtor acquires additional property, lien can also attach to that new property. 
   - Writ of Execution - Court order judgement debtor's property to be sold in order to satisfy judgment if judgement is not paid off. 
   - If debtor pays off judgment, she should record satisfaction of judgement for constructive notice that lien has been released. 
- Attachment Liens (involuntary) - In lawsuit, if plaintiff is concerned that defendant might try sell any property that could subject to future judgement, plaintiff can ask court to issue writ of attachment. 
   - Provides public notice of lawsuit, so that anyone who bought property from defendent would take title subject to plaintiff's lien. 
   - Lis Pendens - Legal document that notifies anyone who purchases affected proeprty about pending lawsuit. 
- Property Tax Liens (involuntary) - (most common) Also called ad valorem tax lien. Local governments taxing value of real property on an annual basis, if those property taxes become delinquent, property tax lien allows government to foreclose on property. 
- Special Assessments - Tax levied against properties that benefit from public improvment such as paving of street. Creates involuntary, specific lien against each of those properties. 
- IRS Liens (involuntary) - Is a general lien and attaches to everything taxpayer owns, IRS lien attaches only when payment of income taxes is overdue. 
## Priorty and Payment of Liens
If piece of propety has more than one lien against it, and property gets foreclosed on, proceeds may not be enough to pay off all the liens. Then, proceeds are not divided up propotionately, instead, each lien is paid off in order of priority. 
First Lien Position - Lienholder who will be paid first (senior to other junior liens). 
Recording Date - As general rule, lien priority is established by recording date ("First to record, first in right"). 
Tax and Assessment Liens - Property tax liens and special assessment liens are superior to all other liens against property. 
Construction Liens - In Washington, construcion lien's priority is established by date claimant began working on or supplying materials for the project. 
Homestead Law - Provides homeowners some limited protection against lien foreclosure. Homestead if dwelling occupied by owner, plus land and any outbuilding appurtenant to the dwelling. In Washington, homestead protection is automatic. Owner may obtain homestead protection in advance for property she intendes to occupy by recording declaration of homestead, person can only have one homestead at a time. 
- Creates exemption against foreclosure of most judgement liens; however, doesn't protect against forecolsure of voluntary liens. If judgement creditor forecloses, certain amount of homeowner's equity in homestead property is exempt from claim. In Washington, exemption amount is tied to property's location: equal to previous year's median sales prices of single-family home in that county. Judgement creditor can't foreclose on homestead unless property's net value is more than applicable exemption amount. Net value is market value minus total amount senior liens - those that have higher priority thatn lien being foreclosed on. 
## Nonfinancial Encumbrances
Commonly affect both title and use. 
Easement - Right to sue someone else's land for particular purpose. Easement holder has nonpossessory interest in other person's property. 
- Easement or ingress and egress (entering and exiting), it's an access easement allowing landowner to enter and exit his property by corssing neighboring land. 
- Easement Appurtenant - Burdens one piece of land for benefit of another piece of land. Land that receives benefit of easement is called dominant tenement. Land burdended by easement is servient tenement. Runs with land, ordinarily continues to exist even after title to either of the properties changes hands. Usually agreement or deed creating easement must be in writing to be valid and it must be recorded in order to run with the land. However, if use of easement is apparent, it might run with the land even without being written or recorded. 
   - Called easement appurtenant because it goes along with ownership of land, addes value of the land. But can't be severed from land and sold separately, whoever owns dominant tenement owns easement rights. 
- Easement in Gross - Personal rights, not tied to ownership of parcel of land. Benefits a particular person or entity rather than parcel of land. Someone who has easement in gross may be called dominant tenant, but there is no dominant tenement (land). Only property involved is servient tenement, parcel burdened by easement. Burden of easement in gross runs with land. Benefit of easement in gross is considered personal right that can't be assigned to someone else. 
- Commercial Easement in Gross - Held by company for business purposes. Most residential properties burdened by easement held by utility companies, which allow workers to enter property to install and repair utility lines. May be assigned from one entity to another.
